# The Micro-Retirement

### Workers are taking the golden years in installments — and betting the labor market will have them back

**BUSINESS | THE NEW WORKPLACE**

*By Dana Whitfield*
*July 27, 2026*

CHICAGO — Marcus Ellery gave notice on a Tuesday, and by Friday three people had asked him what was wrong.

Nothing was wrong. That was the confusing part. He was 38, eleven years into a career in logistics software, current on a mortgage, married, well reviewed. He had spent three years quietly filling a savings account for exactly this, and in February he emptied part of it and stopped working for four months.

He did not travel. He did not write a novel or start a company or, as his father predicted, lose his mind. He drove his daughter to school. He learned to make bread, badly. He saw a dentist for the first time since 2021. He slept.

"Everybody wanted to know the plan," Mr. Ellery said. "The plan was to stop. That was the whole document."

He is working again, at a competitor, for about 8 percent more than he made before. He is also saving for the next one.

Workers like Mr. Ellery have given the practice a name that irritates economists and delights everyone else: the micro-retirement. Rather than bank four decades of labor against leisure at 67, a growing number of people in their late 20s through their 50s are drawing that leisure down in installments — three months here, a year there — and betting they can cover the interest.

## A gap year for grown-ups

The idea is not new. Academia has had sabbaticals for a century, and Silicon Valley has long dangled them in front of senior engineers as a retention device. What has changed is who takes them, and why.

A survey conducted this spring by the Cordera Institute, a labor research nonprofit, found that 19 percent of full-time American workers between 25 and 55 had taken a voluntary break of two months or longer since 2020 — close to double the share reporting the same in a comparable 2016 survey. Among workers under 40, it was 24 percent.

The important detail is in the word voluntary. These were not layoffs, illnesses or caregiving crises. These were people who quit on purpose, intending to come back.

"The traditional model assumes your health, your family's need for you and your appetite for adventure all peak in the same year, and that the year is 65," said Priya Raghunathan, an economist at the University of Michigan who studies career interruptions. "For most people that is plainly false. The micro-retirement is a correction to a badly designed schedule."

Dr. Raghunathan is precise about who gets to make that correction. Her data show the practice clustered among salaried workers with portable skills, no employer-dependent visa status and no chronic condition requiring uninterrupted coverage. Hourly workers, single parents and anyone whose green card is stapled to a job appear almost nowhere in the numbers.

"This is a real story about a narrow slice of the labor market," she said. "It is not a story about the labor market."

## The math nobody wants to do

The cost is not simply forgone salary. In the United States it is health coverage, retirement contributions, the employer match, vesting cliffs — and the compounding all of it would have done for the next thirty years.

Financial planners have begun building the scenario directly into their software. Elena Marchetti, a certified financial planner in Denver, now runs a career-gap projection for roughly one client in six under 50.

Her arithmetic for a 35-year-old earning $120,000 who takes six months: about $60,000 in salary, roughly $9,000 in missed employer contributions, and — if that $9,000 had grown at 7 percent until retirement — something near $68,000 in future money that will simply never exist.

"About half of them look at that number and decide against it," Ms. Marchetti said. "The other half say, fine, I'll work an extra year at the end. That is a perfectly rational trade. It just isn't the trade the industry trained them to make."

The trade sours with age. A break at 52 is harder to recover from than one at 32, financially and in the hiring market, where managers reliably read an older applicant's gap as a warning and a younger one's as a story.

## What employers figured out

For years the advice was to hide the gap, or dress it up as consulting. That advice is decaying, unevenly.

Nine of the 40 large employers contacted for this article said they now offer unpaid leave of three months or longer to employees below the executive level, up from three in 2019. Almost none framed it as generosity.

"Replacing a mid-level engineer runs north of a year of their salary once you count recruiting and ramp-up," said the chief people officer at a Midwestern insurance company, granted anonymity to discuss internal cost figures. "Holding a seat open for four months costs a quarter of that. This is not a values statement. It's arithmetic."

The view from the middle is less tidy. Managers described covering absences on teams already running thin, and the politics of deciding who gets a yes.

"It's a benefit that flows to people who are already doing well," said a director at a health care company. "Nobody is approving four months for the person on a performance plan."

## The part that surprises everyone

Nearly every person interviewed described the same arc, and almost none of them saw it coming.

The first weeks are euphoric. The middle is not. Somewhere around week six, the scaffolding a job had been quietly providing — a reason to leave the house, a set of people who noticed whether you did — comes down, and the silence is louder than anticipated.

"I thought I was tired," said Nell Ortiz, 44, a nurse practitioner in Tucson who took five months off last year. "I was tired. I was also lonely, and I'd been confusing the two for about three years."

Ms. Ortiz returned to clinical work in the fall, four days a week instead of five. She calls the break the most useful thing she has ever done, and says she would not do it the same way twice.

"I'd plan the middle," she said. "Everyone plans the first week."

That is the advice that keeps surfacing: give the time a shape, and fix the end date before the start date. The people who drifted without one described a job search that began later and went worse than it should have.

## The bet underneath

Strip away the language and a micro-retirement is a wager on the future — that the market will still want you in six months, that your skills will not have gone stale, that the uninsured stretch will not be the stretch in which something is found.

For several years running, that wager has mostly paid. In a weaker labor market it would look considerably less clever, and the people now writing about it approvingly would be writing about it as a cautionary tale.

Mr. Ellery understands this. He also knows precisely what he bought. In April he was on his living room floor at two in the afternoon, sorting puzzle pieces by color, when it struck him that he had never once been in that room at that hour on a Tuesday.

"I've got maybe eleven or twelve years where she wants to do puzzles with me," he said. "I've got thirty where I can work. I don't understand why we all agreed to take those in that order."
