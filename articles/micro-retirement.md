# The Micro-Retirement: Why Workers Are Taking Their Golden Years in Installments

**BUSINESS | THE NEW WORKPLACE**

*By Dana Whitfield*
*July 27, 2026*

---

CHICAGO — In February, Marcus Ellery did something his father called insane. Eleven years into a career in logistics software, with a mortgage and a five-year-old, he gave notice, drained a savings account he had spent three years filling, and did nothing in particular for four months.

He did not travel the world. He did not write a novel. He took his daughter to school every morning, learned to make bread badly, saw a dentist for the first time since 2021, and slept.

"People kept asking what my plan was," Mr. Ellery, 38, said. "The plan was to stop. That was the entire plan."

He is back at work now, at a competitor, for about 8 percent more than he was making before. And he is already saving for the next one.

Workers like Mr. Ellery have given the practice a name that irritates economists and delights everyone else: the micro-retirement. Instead of banking decades of labor in exchange for leisure at 67, a growing number of people in their late 20s through their 50s are withdrawing small installments of that leisure along the way — three months here, a year there — and betting they can afford the interest.

## A gap year for grown-ups

The idea is not new. Sabbaticals have existed in academia for a century, and Silicon Valley has long offered them to senior engineers as a retention perk. What has changed is who is taking them and why.

A survey conducted this spring by the Cordera Institute, a labor research nonprofit, found that 19 percent of full-time American workers between 25 and 55 had taken a voluntary break of two months or longer at some point since 2020 — nearly double the share who reported the same in a comparable 2016 survey. Among workers under 40, the figure was 24 percent.

Crucially, most were not fired, not sick and not caring for a relative. They quit on purpose, with the intention of returning.

"The traditional model assumes your health, your family's need for you, and your appetite for adventure all peak at the same moment, which is somehow age 65," said Priya Raghunathan, an economist at the University of Michigan who studies career interruptions. "For most people that is obviously false. The micro-retirement is a correction to a badly designed schedule."

Dr. Raghunathan is careful to note who can do this. Her research finds that the practice is concentrated among salaried workers with portable skills, no employer-dependent visa status and no chronic medical condition requiring continuous insurance. "This is a story about a specific slice of the labor market," she said. "It's a real story, but it is not everyone's story."

## The math nobody wants to do

The cost is not just forgone salary. In the United States, it is health coverage, retirement contributions, employer matches, vesting schedules and the compounding those contributions would have done for 30 years.

Financial planners have started building the scenario into their software. Elena Marchetti, a certified financial planner in Denver, said she now runs a "career gap" projection for roughly one in six clients under 50.

Her rule of thumb: a six-month break at age 35 for a worker earning $120,000 costs about $60,000 in salary, $9,000 in missed employer contributions, and — if that $9,000 had grown at 7 percent for three decades — roughly $68,000 in retirement money that will never exist.

"When I show people that number, about half of them decide against it," Ms. Marchetti said. "The other half say, 'Fine, I'll work an extra year at the end.' And honestly? That's a rational trade. It just isn't the trade the industry taught them to make."

The trade gets worse the later it happens. A break at 52 is harder to recover from, both financially and in the labor market, where hiring managers reliably treat older applicants' gaps with more suspicion than younger ones'.

## What employers are learning

For years the standing advice was to hide the gap or dress it as consulting. That advice is decaying, if unevenly.

Nine of the 40 large employers contacted for this article said they now offer unpaid leave of three months or longer to employees below the executive level, up from three that offered it in 2019. Several described it, bluntly, as cheaper than turnover.

"Replacing a mid-level engineer costs us somewhere north of a year of their salary once you count recruiting and ramp," said the chief people officer at a Midwestern insurance company, who was granted anonymity to discuss internal cost figures. "Holding a seat empty for four months costs us a quarter of that. It is not generosity. It is arithmetic."

Not everyone is convinced. Managers interviewed described the strain of covering absences on teams already running lean, and the awkward politics of who gets approved. "It's a benefit that goes to the people who are already doing well," one director at a health care company said. "Nobody's granting a four-month sabbatical to the person on a performance plan."

## The part that surprises people

Almost everyone interviewed for this article described the same arc, and almost none of them expected it.

The first weeks are euphoric. The middle is uncomfortable. Somewhere around week six, the structure that a job had been quietly providing — a reason to leave the house, a set of people who noticed whether you did — goes missing, and the absence is louder than anticipated.

"I thought I was tired," said Nell Ortiz, 44, a nurse practitioner in Tucson who took five months off last year. "I was tired. But I was also lonely, and I had confused the two for about three years."

Ms. Ortiz went back to clinical work in the fall, at four days a week instead of five. She calls the break the most useful thing she has ever done and says she would not repeat it the same way. "I'd plan the middle part," she said. "Everyone plans the first week."

That is the advice that recurs: treat the time like a project with a shape, and set the end date before the start date. Those who drifted without one described a slow slide into a job search that began later and went worse than it should have.

## The bet underneath

What the micro-retirement really represents is a wager about the future — that the labor market will still want you when you come back, that your skills will not have rotted, that the health insurance gap will not coincide with the diagnosis.

For the last several years, that wager has mostly paid off. In a slacker market, it would look different.

Mr. Ellery knows this. He also knows what he bought. In April he was on the floor of his living room at two in the afternoon, assembling a puzzle with his daughter, and it occurred to him that he had never once been in that room at that hour on a Tuesday.

"I have maybe eleven or twelve years where she wants to do puzzles with me," he said. "I have thirty years where I can work. I don't understand why we all agreed to spend those in the wrong order."
