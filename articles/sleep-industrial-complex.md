# You Are Not Sleeping Badly. You Are Being Told You Are.

**HEALTH | WELL**

*By Miriam Solberg*
*July 27, 2026*

---

Every morning at 6:40, before coffee, before speaking to another human being, Jordan Pike receives a number. It is between 1 and 100. It is called a Recovery Score, and it is generated by a band on his wrist that has spent the night measuring his heart rate variability, respiration and movement.

On a good morning the number is in the 80s and Mr. Pike, 41, a physical therapist in Sacramento, feels capable. On a bad morning it is 34, and he has noticed something that troubles him.

"On a 34 day I'm tired all day," he said. "Even if I slept fine. Especially if I slept fine and it says 34. Then I spend the whole day trying to figure out what's wrong with me."

Mr. Pike has since stopped looking at the number before noon. His sleep, by his own account, has improved.

There is now a clinical term for the thing he was doing to himself. In 2017, researchers at Rush University Medical Center described a pattern of patients arriving with insomnia complaints driven not by poor sleep but by anxiety about data suggesting poor sleep. They named it orthosomnia — an unhealthy preoccupation with achieving perfect sleep.

Nearly a decade later, the trackers have gotten far better and the problem has gotten considerably bigger.

## An enormous market built on a real problem

To be clear about what is not in dispute: sleep matters, most adults need roughly seven to nine hours, and a large share of Americans do not get it. Chronic short sleep is associated with cardiovascular disease, metabolic dysfunction, impaired immune response and worse mental health. Untreated obstructive sleep apnea is genuinely dangerous and genuinely underdiagnosed.

Consumer sleep technology is now a multibillion-dollar global category, spanning rings, bands, mattresses that change temperature, apps that score you, weighted blankets, mouth tape, red-light bulbs and a supplement aisle that did not exist in 2015.

Some of it works. A great deal of it has been tested primarily by the companies selling it.

"Wearables are quite good at telling you when you were asleep versus awake," said Dr. Nadia Ferreira, a sleep physician at a Boston academic medical center. "They are mediocre at staging — telling you how much deep or REM sleep you got. Validation studies against polysomnography show the stage-level agreement is often in the 50-to-70-percent range. And yet the stage numbers are exactly what the app puts on the front screen, in a nice color, with a little trend arrow."

Her objection is not that the devices are useless. It is that precision is being displayed where precision does not exist. "A number with two significant figures reads as a fact," she said. "It's an estimate with a wide error bar wearing a costume."

## The rebound effect

The mechanism by which sleep tracking backfires is well understood, because it is the same mechanism behind ordinary insomnia.

Sleep requires a reduction in arousal. Monitoring performance increases arousal. So a person who lies down thinking *I need to hit 90 minutes of deep sleep tonight* has, in the act of thinking it, made that outcome less likely — and then receives a report in the morning confirming the failure, which supplies material for the following night's worry.

"It's a closed loop, and the device is inside it," said Dr. Ferreira. "I have patients who sleep well and feel terrible, and the only variable I can find is the app."

The gold-standard treatment for chronic insomnia is not a supplement or a device. It is cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia, or CBT-I, which multiple professional bodies — including the American College of Physicians — recommend as first-line care ahead of medication. It typically runs four to eight sessions. Several of its core instructions are the direct opposite of what a tracking app encourages: get out of bed when you cannot sleep, stop watching the clock, restrict time in bed rather than extending it, and abandon the goal of controlling sleep.

Access remains the obstacle. Trained CBT-I clinicians are scarce, and waitlists in many regions run for months, which is part of why an $89 ring feels like the available option.

## What the evidence actually supports

Strip away the marketing and the interventions with the strongest research behind them are unglamorous, free and repetitive:

**A fixed wake time, seven days a week.** The single most effective lever most people have, and the one most often sacrificed on weekends. Wake time anchors the circadian clock; bedtime largely follows it.

**Morning light, outdoors.** Ten to thirty minutes shortly after waking. Outdoor light on an overcast day is dramatically brighter than any indoor room, and the effect is on timing, not just alertness.

**The bed reserved for sleep and sex.** Working, scrolling and worrying in bed teach the brain that the bed is a place for those activities.

**Caffeine cut roughly eight to ten hours before bed,** and alcohol treated as what it is — a sedative that reliably fragments the second half of the night and suppresses REM, which is why six hours after three drinks feels like four.

**A cool, dark room.** Core body temperature must fall for sleep onset; a warm bedroom is a common and easily fixed obstacle.

None of these can be purchased, which is roughly the point.

## When to stop optimizing and see a doctor

Dr. Ferreira's list of things no app can fix: loud snoring with witnessed pauses in breathing; falling asleep involuntarily during the day; an irresistible urge to move the legs at night; difficulty sleeping three or more nights a week for longer than three months. Those are clinical presentations, and several of them have excellent treatments.

"If your device tells you something is wrong and you feel fine, the device is the thing to investigate," she said. "If you feel wrong, I don't care what the device says. Come in."

Mr. Pike still wears his band. He looks at the weekly average, not the daily score, which is closer to how the underlying data should be read anyway. Mostly he has gone back to a test he used before he owned it.

"I ask myself if I'm tired," he said. "It turns out I can tell."
