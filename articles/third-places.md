# Where Did Everyone Go? The Quiet Collapse of the Place You Used to Hang Out

**CULTURE | AMERICAN LIFE**

*By Owen Braddock*
*July 27, 2026*

---

DAYTON, Ohio — The bowling alley on Watervliet Avenue closed on a Sunday in March, after 61 years, and the last league to use it — eight men whose average age was 63 — spent the following Tuesday sitting in a Bob Evans parking lot in their cars, deciding what to do.

"We tried a place out on the highway," said Ron Kessler, 66, a retired machinist and the league's unofficial secretary. "It's fine. It's got the lanes. But you don't run into anybody there. You go, you bowl, you leave."

The distinction Mr. Kessler is making — between a place you go to do a thing and a place where you run into people — is the one the sociologist Ray Oldenburg drew in 1989 when he coined the term "third place." Not home, not work, but the third thing: the barbershop, the diner counter, the corner pub, the church hall, the bowling alley on Watervliet Avenue.

Those places have been thinning out for 40 years. What has changed recently is the pace, and who notices.

## The numbers behind the feeling

Between 1998 and 2024, the number of bowling centers in the United States fell by roughly half. Bars and taverns, as a share of food-and-drink establishments, have declined steadily since the 1980s. Religious congregation membership crossed below 50 percent of American adults in 2020 for the first time since Gallup began asking in 1937. Fraternal organizations — the Elks, the Moose, the Kiwanis — have lost most of a generation.

Meanwhile, the American Time Use Survey shows time spent socializing in person falling in nearly every age bracket, with the steepest drop among adults in their 20s, who now log substantially less face-to-face social time per day than the same age group did in 2003.

"People hear these statistics and reach for a villain — phones, usually," said Camille Duquette, a sociologist at Rutgers who studies neighborhood institutions. "Phones matter. But I'd point at something less interesting: the price of commercial real estate, the collapse of the informal economy that let a guy run a bar as a break-even hobby, and the fact that we zoned most of the country so you cannot walk to anything."

Dr. Duquette's point is that third places were rarely profitable in the way modern retail must be. They survived on low rent, cash businesses, owners who lived upstairs and customers who came four nights a week. Remove any of those legs and the stool falls.

## The replacement that isn't

Something has moved into the vacancy: the third place that charges admission.

Boutique fitness studios, run clubs, climbing gyms, board-game cafes, co-working lounges and $9 pour-over counters have all been described, often by their own marketing departments, as community. Some genuinely are. Nearly all of them share a trait the old ones did not: you must keep paying.

"I love my gym. I have four real friends from my gym," said Aisha Nolan, 31, a project manager in Atlanta. "It's $189 a month. When I got laid off in 2023, I cancelled it, and I lost the four friends in about six weeks. That's when I understood what I'd actually been buying."

The economics select for a particular kind of person — one with disposable income, a flexible evening and, frequently, no children. The people historically most served by third places, retirees and shift workers and teenagers, are the ones least served by the paid version.

Public libraries are the significant counterexample, and librarians know it. Visits for purposes unrelated to borrowing books — meetings, classes, warmth, company, air-conditioning, Wi-Fi — now make up a large and growing share of foot traffic. "We're the last building in town where you can sit for four hours and nobody asks you to buy anything," said a branch manager in suburban Cleveland. "We were not staffed for that. We do it anyway."

## What actually gets lost

The consequences show up in places that seem unrelated.

Loneliness has been the headline, and it is real: the U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory linked chronic social disconnection to cardiovascular risk on a scale comparable to well-known behavioral risk factors. But researchers who study civic life point to a second loss that is harder to feel and possibly more consequential.

Third places produced weak ties — the acquaintances who are not your friends. Weak ties are how people historically found jobs, apartments, contractors, babysitters and, in the classic sociological finding, spouses. They are also, less romantically, the mechanism by which people encounter neighbors who disagree with them and continue to think of those neighbors as people.

"Strong ties give you support. Weak ties give you a society," Dr. Duquette said. "We have gotten reasonably good at preserving the first and terrible at the second."

## Small, unglamorous fixes

There is no policy that reopens the bowling alley on Watervliet Avenue. But a handful of interventions keep appearing in the research and in interviews, and none of them are grand.

Zoning that permits a coffee shop on a residential corner. Municipal support for the buildings that already function this way — libraries, rec centers, senior centers — rather than new "innovation districts." Recurring, unstructured events on a fixed schedule, which turn out to matter more than the quality of the event itself. And, repeatedly, someone willing to be the person who sends the text.

That last one is doing a great deal of work. In interviews across four states, nearly every functioning informal group traced back to a single individual who had appointed themselves the organizer, usually without being asked and often while quietly resenting it.

"It's me. I'm the one who books it," said Devon Marsh, 47, who has convened a Thursday pickup basketball game in Portland, Me., for nine years. "If I stopped, it would end within a month. I've tested this theory by getting the flu."

Mr. Kessler's league, in Dayton, found a new home in June — a VFW hall with four lanes in the basement and a bar that opens at four. Attendance is up. Two of the men have started coming early to eat dinner there.

"It's not the same building," Mr. Kessler said. "But somebody knew my name on the second week. That's the whole thing, isn't it. That's all it ever was."
